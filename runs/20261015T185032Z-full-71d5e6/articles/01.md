# Open weights, different doors: what you can actually use now

A model can be “available” without being easy to use. Some releases let people download model files; others offer a browser demo, a paid API, or access inside a subscription. The recent releases from Liquid AI, Meta, TII and Google show why those routes—and the equipment needed to run a model—matter as much as the announcement itself.

## Downloading is not the same as deploying

Liquid AI says its LFM2.5-24B-A2B reasoning model and its base version are open-weight and available on Hugging Face. That means the learned model parameters can be downloaded. It does not, by itself, validate the company’s performance claims or mean that the complete software and training process are open source.[^1]<!-- claim:01-c1 -->

Meta’s Muse Spark Open offers another kind of downloadable model. A Hugging Face article describes it as accepting text, images, audio and video, while generating text. It reports approximately 816 billion total parameters, with about 42 billion active for each token the model processes.[^2]<!-- claim:01-c2 --> A “mixture of experts” design routes each token through only some of a model’s component networks, or experts. This reduces the computation required for each token, but does not necessarily make the full model small enough to store on ordinary hardware.

That distinction is central to Muse’s practical requirements. The article says eight experts are selected for each token, but all 256 experts still need to remain in memory. It lists the BF16 checkpoint—the model files in a common numerical format—as about 1.5 terabytes.[^3]<!-- claim:01-c3 --> The same article describes quantized versions and a test on a Mac with 512 GB of memory, but that is still far from a typical personal computer. Download access is real; convenient local deployment is another question.

## A demo, an API and a subscription

TII’s Falcon-ASR, a speech-recognition model with a particular focus on Arabic, is accessible through a Hugging Face demo that lets people try transcriptions. But TII describes API access and native applications as planned, not already launched.[^4]<!-- claim:01-c4 --> A demo can show how a model responds to a recording, but is not equivalent to integrating it into an application or running it at scale.

Google’s Gemini 4 Argon illustrates a different route. Google announced global availability for developers and enterprises through the API in AI Studio and the Gemini platform. It also listed access for Google AI Ultra subscribers in Antigravity, and for Ultra and Pro subscribers in the Gemini app and AI Mode in Search.[^5]<!-- claim:01-c5 --> Those are specific developer, enterprise and paid-consumer routes, not unrestricted access for every user. The announcement establishes what Google says is available, not an independent audit of who can successfully reach the service in every region or account.

One creator’s early-access video offers a small, practical glimpse of Argon, but not a controlled test. Matt Wolfe reports that the model generated a 3D bust that he printed, while two task workflows appeared to stall. One task had produced a slide deck despite remaining stuck in the interface; the other had shown no plan. Wolfe suggested the early version might have bugs.[^6]<!-- claim:01-c6 --> That mixed experience matters as a reminder that access to a preview can expose real capabilities and unfinished product behavior at the same time. A single creator’s demo cannot tell us how the model performs consistently, or whether the failures would recur in a general release.

## Compare the route, not just the label

These cases form a spectrum. Liquid AI’s announced downloadable weights provide one kind of access; Muse’s weights come with unusually large memory requirements; Falcon-ASR offers a try-it demo while its API remains planned. Google’s announcement instead describes routes through paid products and developer platforms.[^7]<!-- claim:01-c7 --> “Open-weight” therefore says something important about whether parameters are released, but it does not answer whether a reader has the hardware, software, or account needed to put a model to work.

The comparison also has limits. Most access statements here come from the companies or platforms involved, rather than independent availability audits. The Argon video is anecdotal, and its early-access status may help explain the stalled workflows; it does not establish a benchmark result. Nor do these access details establish performance parity or the cost of operating the models in typical settings.

For readers choosing what to try, the useful first question is practical: do you need files to run locally, a demo to explore, an API to build with, or a model inside a subscription? The evidence here can identify those doors and some of the equipment behind them. It cannot yet say which door will prove most useful to different users.
