# What the new AI evaluations show—and what they leave out

A benchmark score can look like a simple answer to “How good is this model?” In reality, it answers a narrower question: how a system performed on a particular task, dataset, and scoring method. Recent results from a language model, a speech recognizer, a climate emulator, and a robotics system make that distinction especially important. They are not contestants in one shared race.

## One language model, different task results

Liquid AI describes LFM2.5-24B-A2B as a mixture-of-experts model, a design that divides work among specialized components. Its stated architecture has 40 layers and 64 experts in each mixture-of-experts block, with top-four routing. Put simply, the system selects a small group of experts to process each token rather than sending every token through every expert. Liquid AI says the model has 24 billion total parameters but activates two billion per token.[^1]<!-- claim:02-c1 -->

That design is relevant to the company’s stated interest in efficient agent use, but it does not settle how well the model performs across tasks. Liquid AI’s table compares it with three named models on 16 benchmarks. Its reported LiveClaw score is ahead of those listed competitors. On GPQA Diamond and SWE-Bench Verified, however, it trails Qwen3.6. These are comparisons under the company’s stated conditions: the models were evaluated with vLLM and benchmark-recommended generation settings. The results point to differing strengths, not an all-purpose ranking.[^2]<!-- claim:02-c2 -->

A benchmark is a defined test set and scoring procedure. It can help readers compare systems on the tested task, but it does not cover every use a person might make of a model. A strong result on an agentic or tool-use test, for example, does not by itself establish equally strong performance on coding or general knowledge.

## Speech recognition, with a dialect-specific question

TII’s Falcon-ASR targets speech recognition, with particular attention to Arabic and Emirati speech. TII reports an average word error rate of 20.92% across six public Arabic test sets, and a 22.73% word error rate on a separate internal Emirati evaluation. Word error rate measures transcription mistakes relative to the reference words; lower values indicate fewer errors. These are TII-reported results, and the internal dialect evaluation should not be conflated with the public leaderboard comparison. The test sets and evaluation conditions differ.[^3]<!-- claim:02-c3 -->

That distinction matters because speech quality is not one uniform property. A test focused on Arabic speech and a separate evaluation focused on Emirati speech address related but distinct questions. The figures help explain what the developer tested; they do not guarantee the same accuracy for every speaker, recording condition, or dialect.

## Climate detail tied to a particular simulation

Ai2’s HiRO-ACEv2 addresses yet another task. Its system combines ACE, which operates at coarse resolution, with HiROv2, which downscales outputs into finer detail. Ai2 says the updated HiROv2 can predict several high-resolution fields, including rain, wind, and pressure, and that its statistics match the X-SHiELD target simulation within 3%, even for events described as extremely rare. Those are Ai2-reported results, not an independent replication.[^4]<!-- claim:02-c4 -->

A close statistical match to a reference simulation is meaningful within that comparison, but it is not evidence that the system reproduces every real-world climate outcome. The authors state that HiRO currently depends on an ACE version tuned to X-SHiELD. The scope of the comparison is therefore bound to that system and its reference data.

## Robotics: a technical account, not a test result

In a podcast interview, Dyna Robotics co-founder Jason Ma describes Dyna-2 as taking video, robot-joint information, and language instructions as inputs, then producing robot actions and predicted future video. The predicted video is a representation of what the system expects to happen; the action output is intended to control the robot. This description helps explain the design, but an interview with a company representative does not independently verify performance.[^5]<!-- claim:02-c5 -->

Across these examples, the evaluation questions are different: tool use and coding, speech transcription, climate-simulation statistics, and robot action. Their datasets, conditions, and reference points are not interchangeable. The reasonable conclusion is bounded: results support comparisons within the tests described, not a common ranking of all four systems. That conclusion remains limited by the supplied evidence; independent replications and deployment results could change how much confidence readers place in each report.[^6]<!-- claim:02-c6 -->

The useful question is not simply which system has the biggest score. It is what was measured, against what reference, and under which conditions—and whether those conditions resemble the situation where someone hopes to use it.
