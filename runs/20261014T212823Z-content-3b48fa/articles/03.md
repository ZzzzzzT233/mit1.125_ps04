# The next AI bottleneck may be the system around the model

A model’s capabilities are only part of what determines whether it is useful. Recent examples in software engineering, robotics, climate research, and biology point to another question: what data, infrastructure, expertise, and operating conditions are needed around the model? These cases do not establish a universal rule, but they show why model scores alone can miss practical constraints.

## When code production changes the tools around it

Simon Willison reports figures from Anthropic’s account of its engineering work: the company averaged about 2,500 merged code changes per day in May 2026 and reported more than 30,000 per day in September. Those are Anthropic’s figures, relayed by Willison, and are not independently verified in the reporting considered here.[^1]<!-- claim:03-c1 -->

Willison suggests that newer Fable/Mythos-class models may have contributed to the increase, but explicitly calls that explanation his guess. He also cautions that public model release dates do not establish when models became available for Anthropic’s internal use. The proposed connection is therefore not a demonstrated cause-and-effect result. Alongside the reported throughput, Anthropic described building custom source-control tools, an example of adapting engineering infrastructure to its own workflow. But the available account does not isolate which changes produced the higher rate.[^2]<!-- claim:03-c2 -->

That distinction matters. A rise in code output may create new demands on review and coordination, but the reported figures alone do not tell us whether the model, new tools, organizational changes, or some combination explains it. The systems around a model may change as its use changes, while the evidence may not yet identify the contribution of each part.

## Robotics depends on data that fits the hardware

Dyna Robotics co-founder Jason Ma describes a robotics approach combining diverse human video with robot-specific data. Human recordings can cover varied physical activities, he argues, but human movement does not directly map onto the company’s robot hardware. In this account, robot-specific data is needed to adapt the model to the robot’s own actions and body. This is a company representative’s description of the approach, not an independent finding.[^3]<!-- claim:03-c3 -->

Ma also identifies hardware stability and data collection as constraints on broader deployment. His example is the difficulty of evaluating models on physical equipment if the hardware cannot operate reliably or provide suitable opportunities to collect data. These are his views in an interview, not independently established results. Even so, the account makes clear why a robotics system cannot be judged only by what its model predicts: sensors, actuators, data, and the ability to test on real hardware all matter to the company’s stated work.[^4]<!-- claim:03-c4 -->

## Climate simulations have boundaries, too

Ai2’s HiROv2 illustrates a parallel limitation in climate research. The system generates high-resolution detail from a climate simulation, and Ai2 reports close statistical agreement with X-SHiELD. But Ai2 says HiROv2 currently depends on an ACE system tuned to X-SHiELD and cannot yet produce high-resolution results for other climates, including a warmer future. The strength of the reported match and the boundary on its use belong in the same account: a model can perform well against its training-linked reference while still having a limited scope.[^5]<!-- claim:03-c5 -->

This is not just a technical caveat. A climate emulator’s output can only answer questions its underlying system and evidence support.

## Start with the problem, not a favored tool

A podcast discussion featuring participants from Google DeepMind and Biohub offers a useful lens on these examples. A participant argues that scientific work should start with the problem and then determine whether progress requires modeling, more data, or relevant expertise. That is an expert perspective, not a new biological result. The point is that a team should not assume in advance that scaling a model—or generating more data—is the right answer for every scientific question.[^6]<!-- claim:03-c6 -->

Taken together, the cases suggest that practical progress can depend on adapting the workflow around a model: engineering tools for the volume and form of work, data that matches a robot and its sensors, or a simulation system whose limits are understood. This is a synthesis across examples, not evidence that all AI fields face the same bottleneck or are converging on one technique.

The strongest counterargument is that these are very different settings, and several of the claims come from interested organizations or company representatives. Anthropic’s figures are company-reported; Dyna’s account comes from its co-founder; and Ai2’s performance claims are developer-reported. The biology discussion is an interview perspective. None establishes how common such constraints are across the field.

A more useful question than whether models alone are getting stronger may be: what evidence shows that the surrounding data, tools, and hardware are keeping pace—and who has independently tested that whole system?
