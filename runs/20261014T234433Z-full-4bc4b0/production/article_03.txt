# The Hard Part Starts After the Model Works

A model can work and still leave the hardest problem unsolved. Software teams may need better ways to review a surge of changes. Researchers may need suitable data or domain expertise more than another training run. A robot must contend not only with its model, but also with sensors, hardware and the physical environment. Recent company reports and interviews point toward a practical question: what is actually limiting the work once a model is in the picture?

## More generated code, more pressure on the surrounding tools

Simon Willison reports Anthropic’s figure of about 2,500 code changes merged per day in May 2026, rising to more than 30,000 per day by September. He explicitly labels his suggested explanation—that a new class of models is a key factor—as a guess. The figures are reported from the company’s account, and the proposed cause is not established by them. [^1]

Willison also reports that Anthropic built Scion for source control and Antique for code review, and that a seven-person team completed the migration from Git in five months. Source control tracks changes to software so people can collaborate and recover earlier versions; code review is the process of checking changes before they are accepted. The account suggests that development infrastructure can itself become a design problem when a team’s workflow changes. It does not show that every organization needs to replace its tools, or that the reported rise in code changes was caused by the new systems. [^2]

A separate Hugging Face post describes a smaller-scale way to keep model development disciplined. The ML Intern author says the workflow asks for a budget before paid jobs, recommends a baseline before training and runs a small test before a larger job. A baseline is a starting result for comparison; a smoke test is a quick check that a process works at all. Those practices can make an experiment easier to interpret and limit wasted effort, but the costs and project outcomes in the post are self-reported. [^3]

## In science, begin with the problem

An interview with DeepMind’s Pushmeet Kohli and Biohub’s Sal Candido turns the same issue toward scientific research. Kohli argues that researchers should not decide in advance that they are “modelers” or “data people.” They should first understand the problem, then decide whether progress calls for modeling, collecting data or bringing in expertise. His view is grounded in examples discussed in the interview, including the different data conditions around protein research and cell genomics; it is a practitioner’s perspective, not new experimental evidence. [^4]

The point is not that models or data matter less. Rather, a scientific objective may expose a missing ingredient that cannot be fixed simply by scaling what is already available. This connects to the ML Intern workflow in a limited but useful way: both accounts resist committing resources before establishing what needs to be measured or improved. One is advice from a scientific interview; the other is a promotional account of projects, so neither independently verifies the other.

## Physical systems add physical constraints

Dyna Robotics co-founder Jason Ma makes a related argument about robotics. He says progress depends on data and hardware as well as models, and that competing strategies for gathering data and building systems remain too early to call. His remarks come from a company interview, not independent verification of deployments or a survey of the field. Still, they make clear why a robot’s performance cannot be understood as a model-only question: the system must interact with real equipment and tasks. [^5]

Another recent report illustrates why deployment descriptions deserve scrutiny. TechCrunch describes a discrepancy between Flock’s CEO, who said the company’s microphones detect only gunshots, and the company’s account, which says its system can also detect events such as fireworks, sideshows and potential car crashes. Without a technical audit, this disagreement does not establish what the system actually detects. It does show why claims about real-world systems should be checked against technical evidence, not inferred from a demonstration or a public description alone. [^6]

## The bottleneck depends on the job

Read together, these examples suggest—without proving a general industry trend—that practical AI work can shift its bottleneck away from model performance and toward infrastructure, evaluation, domain knowledge or hardware. Anthropic’s reported tooling changes concern code workflow; the ML Intern post emphasizes experimental checks and spending controls; the scientific interview stresses problem-specific choices; and Dyna’s co-founder points to physical data and hardware. These are different constraints, drawn from selected company accounts and interviews, not a representative survey. [^7]

That limitation matters. Anthropic’s code-change figures do not independently establish why activity rose. The ML Intern post is promotional, the science discussion is an interview, and Dyna’s deployment claims come from its co-founder. The Flock report describes conflicting statements, not a technical examination. These sources can suggest questions, but they cannot prove that one constraint dominates across AI or that the same approach will work in every field.

A more useful way to discuss progress may be to ask what changed around a model: Did a team improve its review process, collect better-matched data, establish a meaningful baseline, or adapt its hardware to the task? The evidence here suggests those questions are worth asking alongside “How capable is the model?” Which surrounding constraint is the binding one in each case—and what independent evidence would show it has actually been removed?
