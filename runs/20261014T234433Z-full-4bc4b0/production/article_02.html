A benchmark score can sound like a verdict, but it usually answers a narrower question: how did one system perform on one task, under particular test conditions? Recent model reports make that distinction especially important. Liquid AI evaluates tool-using language models, TII tests speech transcription, and Ai2 works on high-resolution weather simulation. Their results may each be useful, but they do not form one league table.

A model’s design shapes its strengths

Liquid AI says its LFM2.5-24B-A2B retains a mixture-of-experts architecture. In plain terms, a mixture of experts has several specialized components, while a routing mechanism selects only some of them for each token. Liquid AI describes 40 layers, 64 experts per block and top-four routing, meaning four experts are selected at a time. The company also says the context window—the amount of material the model can consider in a session—has expanded from 32,000 to 131,000 tokens. These details describe the design; they do not, on their own, tell users how well it will perform in a particular application. [1]

The company’s benchmark table gives a more mixed picture than a single headline score would. Liquid AI reports competitive results on several tool-use and instruction-following tests, but its table also shows other models ahead on knowledge-heavy and coding benchmarks. The company says all models were evaluated with vLLM, a tool for running language models, using each benchmark’s recommended generation settings. That provides some shared test context, but the reported results are still developer-produced and tied to those benchmarks and settings. [2]

This variation is useful, not embarrassing. A system designed for agentic work—where a model takes actions through tools—may be a promising candidate for certain workflows without leading in coding or broad knowledge. The right interpretation is conditional: identify the task, inspect the test, and check whether it resembles the work you actually need done.

Speech recognition depends on the test material

TII’s Falcon-ASR targets a different problem: turning speech into text, with particular attention to Arabic and the Emirati dialect. Word error rate, or WER, measures the share of words that are transcribed incorrectly; lower is better. TII reports a mean Arabic WER of 20.92% across six public test sets. It compares that with 23.17% for the best published result in its September 30, 2026 leaderboard snapshot. This is TII’s account of a defined comparison, not an independent replication. [3]

TII also reports a separate internal Emirati evaluation using held-out recordings and transcripts checked by people. Falcon-ASR’s reported WER there is 22.73%, with a character error rate of 10.19%. But the account available here does not specify the sample size or how representative those recordings are. That makes the result relevant to the tested material while leaving open how it would transfer to other speakers, settings or dialect mixtures. [4]

This is why a result on a public test set and a result on an internal evaluation should not be treated as equivalent evidence. The internal test may examine a useful gap in public coverage, but readers need details about its size and composition to judge how far the finding travels.

Weather simulation is not the same as forecasting

Ai2’s HiRO-ACEv2 addresses yet another task: converting coarser climate-model output into higher-resolution weather detail. Ai2 says its updated HiROv2 model predicts several fields at once, rather than rainfall alone, and improves performance on extreme events. The stated limitation is important: because HiRO learned from X-SHiELD, it currently works only with the ACE version tuned to match that reference simulation. Ai2 reports agreement with X-SHiELD under those conditions, not general skill across every climate or future scenario. [5]

That boundary is part of the result. Matching statistics in a high-resolution simulation can be meaningful for research, but it is not the same as proving forecast accuracy for every region or climate. Ai2 itself says the system cannot yet generate high-resolution data for other climates, including a warmer future or the historical record in ERA5.

Build evaluations around the question

A Hugging Face post by ML Intern’s users offers a practical complement to these larger evaluations. The authors describe a workflow that asks for a baseline before training, a small smoke test to catch basic problems, and a spending cap before paid jobs. A baseline gives a starting point for judging whether training helped; a smoke test checks that the process works before committing to a larger run. The examples are author-reported projects, not independent validation of the agent workflow. [6]

Across the cases, Liquid AI evaluates language-model benchmarks, TII measures transcription errors, and Ai2 compares simulated weather statistics against a named reference. Since tasks, measures and reference conditions differ, these results cannot establish one cross-domain “best model.” That is a strong conclusion for these examples, but it should not be generalized beyond them. The useful question for any evaluation is what it actually measures, what it leaves out, and whether those conditions match the decision a reader wants to make. [7]
